Axis 6 / Section I

Global Climate Action in 2026 - Insights from the UNFCCC Climate Weeks

The Global Climate Action Agenda (GCAA) presents opportunities to advance and accelerate implementation at scale. It is in that spirit that Climate Weeks in Yeosu, Republic of Korea (held in April) and Baku, Azerbaijan (September), brought together a broad range of stakeholders – including national delegations and non‑Party stakeholders (NPS) – with a strong focus on showcasing practical climate solutions, including those linked to unleashing enablers and accelerators.

Discussions showed that climate action is becoming more practical, finance-ready, inclusive and locally relevant. By bringing negotiators into the same rooms as implementers, financiers, knowledge holders, communities and innovators, the events helped translate broad commitments into concrete pathways: bankable pipelines, country platforms, locally led adaptation, high-integrity carbon markets, digital public goods and people-centred solutions.

On climate finance, identifying ways to address persistent readiness and access challenges was a key area of focus. Many discussions underscored that while global climate funds and private investors are keen to support projects, countries often struggle with the early stages – developing robust project concepts, fulfilling complex requirements, or managing risk. Several solutions addressed this “readiness” gap: country-led investment platforms that match projects to funding sources; expanded readiness support from the Green Climate Fund (GCF), GEF and FLRD and bilateral donors and others to help bridge the gap and prepare viable proposals; and initiatives like the Climate Capital Marketplace, which during CW3 convened small island developing states and conflict-affected nations with major climate funders and technical experts.

Data and digital innovation also emerged as key tools for implementation. In various sessions across different themes and topics, participants discussed how technologies like artificial intelligence (AI), satellite monitoring, and digital platforms are being deployed to speed up climate action – from AI-assisted reforestation using drones in Mongolia’s Altai region to digital twins, predictive analytics, machine learning models being actively used to improve efficiency of industrial assets. Hands-on training components, such as guided scenario analysis exercises using a complex economic modelling tool through a user-friendly interface, were widely highlighted as illustrating how digital tools are making advanced analytical capabilities more accessible, while strengthening participants’ capacity to apply these tools, understanding the impacts of response measures, and translating analytical outputs into insights that directly inform policy design and decision-making. At the same time, discussions highlighted that technology is not a silver bullet: digital solutions must be accessible and governed responsibly, with capacity-building to ensure that all countries, including Least Developed Countries (LDCs) and Small Island Developing States (SIDS), can benefit from innovations like AI without exacerbating inequalities.

Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, and in particular the Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism (PACM) under Article 6.4, is moving decisively from framework development toward operational implementation. Across events, Parties, project participants, financial institutions and technical experts highlighted the substantial progress achieved in establishing the rules, procedures and institutional arrangements necessary for participation in the mechanism. Discussions reflected how countries are increasingly advancing domestic readiness measures, including the appointment of designated national authorities (DNAs), submission of Host Party Participation Requirements, development of regulatory and institutional frameworks, and exploration of linkages between domestic carbon pricing instruments and international carbon markets. Collectively, the discussions highlighted growing confidence among Parties and stakeholders that the PACM is evolving into a functional mechanism capable of supporting NDC implementation, mobilizing climate finance, and enabling international cooperation under Article 6.

The Implementation Forums organized during CW3 and CW4 were focused on supporting efforts to accelerate climate action by creating a dedicated space where Parties and NPS could meet on equal footing to exchange practical solutions, align priorities, and collectively discuss how to move from ambition to implementation at scale. Under Axis 6, one of the lead high-impact, scalable solutions emerging from the Implementation Labs and Impact Dialogues is described below:

CW4

Promotion of information integrity on climate change combining research, journalism, media literacy, governance, and AI-enabled fact-checking

The strongest solution emerging from the session was a comprehensive information integrity approach rather than a purely technological response. Tackling climate mis- and disinformation requires investment in research and journalism, youth-led media literacy, platform accountability, fact-checking tools, trustworthy public information systems, and responsible AI governance.

The Global Initiative for Information Integrity on Climate Change was presented as the key implementation vehicle, combining funding for research and journalism, integration of information integrity into climate governance, and communication efforts that translate climate science into accessible and actionable information. The overall objective is to make evidence-based climate information more visible than misleading narratives while preserving public trust in climate science and institutions.

Key implementation value

  • Strengthens public trust in climate science and institutions through fact-checking, journalism, transparency and evidence-based communication
  • Builds resilience against climate misinformation and AI-enabled manipulation through media literacy, platform accountability and AI-assisted detection tools
  • Improves climate action implementation by ensuring policymakers, communities and youth have access to reliable information for decision-making

The United States Climate Alliance (CCSA)

Announced in 2018, CCSA accelerates priority initiatives toward a Climate-Smart Zone, delivering resilience, social development, and broad-based economic growth for the Caribbean. Its central objective is to help transform the region’s economy by fast-tracking sound public and private investment opportunities that support climate action and economic growth through sustainable development.

CCSA connects promising projects, emerging solutions and innovations with funding and investment, provides the data and insights needed to make better decisions, and advocates for the Caribbean’s priorities on the regional and global stage.

The infrastructure available for tracking global trends across the wide range of topics covered in Axis 6 is similarly diverse. Tracking is strongest for financial flows and capacity support. Non-CO₂ emissions give the clearest off-track signal, and several objectives still rely on one indicator or none.

Under Objective 20, data shows that total climate finance passed USD 2 trillion in 2024. Adaptation finance, on the other hand, is fluctuating and has not grown.

There is also progress under Objective 21, with a new indicator showing that 947 of the 2,079 largest listed companies have a net-zero target, and under Objective 22, with the number of countries with green public procurement frameworks increasing from 49 in 2022 to 54 in 2025.

Similarly, indicators under Objectives 23 and 24 show some progress, with carbon pricing revenue rising from USD 25 billion in 2015 to USD 107.6 billion in 2025, and fossil fuel subsidies decreasing from USD 636 in 2024 to 598.5 billion in 2024, though it is still remains quite high.

Objective 25 is clearly off track. Energy sector methane emissions have plateaued at 144 Mt CH4/year in 2024 and 2025, and agricultural nitrous oxide (N₂O) rose from 7.04 in 2022 to 7.57 Mt/year in 2023, despite available abatement solutions.

Under Objective 26, capacity support is growing. Developing countries receiving National Adaptation Plan (NAP) support rose from 89 in 2024 to 92 in 2026, and Green Climate Fund (GCF) readiness funding reached USD 749 million in 2026, up from USD 641.58 million in 2024.

Data under Objective 27 shows that 74 per cent of people had internet and communications technology (ICT) access in 2025. Objective 30 shows a promising rise in the number of countries signing the Declaration on Information Integrity on Climate Change, from eight in 2024 to 28 in 2026.

Axis 6 / Section III

Progress of plans and initiatives

Key trends from NAZCA, highlights of initiative self-tracking progress, case studies and the Plans to Accelerate Solutions (PAS) under Axis 6.

Knowledge production, sharing and capacity-building is the most prominent CCI function under this axis, followed by Implementation of projects and Mobilization of finance and financial approaches. Axis 6 records the highest count of CCIs reporting Mobilization of finance and financial approaches across all six axes, followed by Axis 3. This highlights the importance of financing both in enabling and accelerating climate action and in transforming agri-food systems. Advocacy, policy engagement and awareness-raising is also represented, while Technological research and innovation and Monitoring, reporting and verification appear less frequently.

Capacity-building is the most frequently reported action type, followed by Advocacy and policy engagement, Enhancing collaboration, and Generation of knowledge products. Although only marginally higher than Axes 1 and 4, Axis 6 records the highest absolute number of reported actions, with nine compared with eight in each of the other two axes. It also records the second-highest ratio of reported actions to CCIs. Axis 4 records the highest ratio. These figures highlight the breadth of reported activity under Axis 6 and the role of enabling activities in supporting climate action.

Axis 6 / Section IV

COP 31 Action Agenda

Bridging priorities, investment and delivery from commitments to implementation through a Climate Implementation Bridge

The Climate Implementation Bridge is structured around three components: robust national architecture and capacity enhancement; support for the aggregation and pipeline development; and finance design and capital mobilization.

Also relevant here is to advance delivery of climate finance, while elevating political engagement of Multilateral Development Banks, and to create enabling conditions for systemic climate action, including solutions that support the implementation of the recommendations of the Baku to Belém Roadmap to 1.3T, while encouraging initiatives to continue to fill gaps and strengthen delivery.

Particular attention is also given to mobilizing finance, investment and enabling appropriate and tailored frameworks for zero waste and circular economy solutions through the Zero Waste Package.
