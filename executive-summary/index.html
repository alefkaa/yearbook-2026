The Global Climate Action Agenda

Climate action by cities, regions and other sub national entities, businesses, international organizations and civil society – known as non-Party stakeholders (NPS) in the UNFCCC process – has been instrumental to the advances made in the past decade towards achieving the goals of the Paris Agreement. Both the appointment of Climate High-Level Champions (CHLC) in 2015 and the launch of the Marrakech Partnership for Global Climate Action (MP) the following year helped kickstart a more structured approach to scaling up the global response to climate change, and enabled a better connection between the Convention and the many voluntary and collaborative actions that were already taking place.

In this new stage, the Global Climate Action Agenda (GCAA) is focused on building on the significant progress made over the last 10 years and supporting the implementation of already agreed outcomes from the UNFCCC process, such as the first global stocktake (GST). GCAA is now structured under six thematic axes, each with a set of objectives.

With a strong emphasis on implementation, a guiding principle under the GCAA 2026-2030 is to build on the existing on-going work by means of Plans to Accelerate Solutions (PAS). Each PAS is a concise, collectively developed multistakeholder workplan identifying priority solutions, key actions, responsibilities and milestones to accelerate implementation by 2028 in alignment with the outcomes of GST 1.

NAZCA

Another underlying principle of GCAA 2026-2030 is strengthened transparency of voluntary climate action. Key to this, the Non-State Actors Zone for Climate Action (NAZCA) portal [insert hyperlink] is the main venue through which actors can demonstrate credible progress, highlighting where action is delivering results and where further efforts are needed, and providing greater visibility to the contribution of NPS to the implementation of the Paris Agreement.

In particular, the portal tracks the progress of Cooperative Climate Initiatives (CCIs), whose information is collected through voluntary registration. Defined as voluntary institutional arrangements jointly organized by multiple entities, including Parties and NPS, CCIs include multi-stakeholder partnerships, joint declarations, coalitions, alliances, and other types of voluntary arrangements where multiple organizations work together towards achieving a clearly defined climate-related goal, aligned with the goals of the Paris Agreement and shared by the entities within the CCI. The number of registered CCIs has grown steadily over the years, increasing from 163 in 2023 to 312 in 2026.

The secretariat invites CCIs registered on NAZCA to report progress annually under a common reporting framework, providing registered CCIs with the opportunity to demonstrate key actions undertaken, report progress on self-identified targets, and share challenges and opportunities encountered. The information reported by CCIs strengthens the credibility of their commitments and helps track their progress, contributing to greater transparency and a better understanding of the collective impact of climate action by NPS.

This year, 83 registered CCIs participated in the process and submitted progress information within the requested timeframe, compared to 48 in 2023. While this may be partially due to the increased number of registered CCIs, the growing number of CCIs reporting progress can be considered as encouraging in the context of transparent climate action.

The Yearbook of Global Climate Action

First launched in 2017, the Yearbook of Global Climate Action is a key tool developed by the CHLC, with the support of the UNFCCC secretariat, to track progress, impacts and results in the implementation of climate action, and to identify best practices and lessons learned.

The 2025 Yearbook launched the GCAA’s new structure, with chapters covering each of the six axes and presenting a trend analysis of global progress under the axes’ objectives in the past decade, based on a set of tailored global socio-economic indicators (GSIs).

The full edition of the 2026 Yearbook – celebrating both the tenth iteration of its series and the Marrakech Partnership’s 10-year anniversary – is now available as a webpage [insert hyperlink] . It builds on the structure and contents of the 2025 edition, with dedicated chapters covering the six axes under the GCAA. Each chapter provides an update of the GSIs for comparability, where indicators are assigned one of three states based on its direction of travel between the value reported in the 2025 Yearbook and the latest available data: progress, where the latest value has moved in the desired direction; no progress, where it is flat or has moved the wrong way; or data insufficient, where there is no comparable baseline. The chapters also provide an overview of CCIs progress as reported to NAZCA under the common reporting framework, and a list of PAS per axis.

For the purposes of this Executive Summary, the information provided in the full edition was summarized in a table for each axis, providing an overview of global progress and remaining gaps as identified by the GSIs, as well as information on CCIs and PAS.

Axis 1. Transitioning Energy, Industry, and Transport

Of the 26 global indicators under this axis: 17 show progress | 9 show no progress

Global Progress

  • Seventeen per cent increase in renewable energy capacity between 2024 and 2025
  • Road transport is electrifying quickly: electric vehicles made up a quarter of light-duty sales in 2025, while public charging points reached more than seven million and zero-emission heavy-duty vehicle sales jumped from 1.8 per cent in 2024 to nine per cent in 2025
  • The number of people without electricity and without clean cooking keep falling steadily, albeit below what is necessary – with the latter reaching a decrease of nearly 26 per cent between 2023 and 2024
  • Explicit fossil fuel subsidies fell from USD 1.4 trillion in 2022 to USD 0.725 trillion in 2024

Remaining Gaps

  • The rate of improvement of energy intensity remained at one per cent in 2024 (against the four per cent a year needed by 2030)
  • The carbon intensity of steel production also stayed flat from last check (2023) at 1,920 kgCO₂ per tonne in 2024
  • Oil & gas methane emissions increased from 71.5 Mt CH₄ in 2024 to around 72.3 Mt CH₄ in 2025, while oil and gas production covered by methane pledges fell from 80 per cent in 2024 to ~75 per cent in 2025

Axis 2. Stewarding Forests, Oceans and Biodiversity

Of the 11 global indicators under this axis: 5 show progress | 2 show no progress | 4 have insufficient data

Global Progress

  • Deforestation slowed in 2025 (nearly 14 per cent decrease in comparison with 2024)
  • 11.8 million hectares are under active restoration with projects in 2026 (up from 10.6 Mha in 2024)
  • Marine protected areas reached 10 per cent in 2025 (up from 8.5 per cent in 2024 and from 4.1 per cent in 2015)

Remaining Gaps

  • Forest degradation is still rising (from 63 Mha in 2022 to 65.4 Mha in 2024)
  • There are currently no global indicators tracking biodiversity recovery or desertification – a key gap for Rio Synergies

Axis 3. Transforming Agriculture and Food Systems

Of the 10 global indicators under this axis: 5 show progress | 4 show no progress | 1 has insufficient data

Global Progress

  • Emissions intensity declined for enteric fermentation and rice cultivation between 2022 and 2023, while ruminant meat productivity rose from 30 to 31 kg per hectare between 2022 and 2024
  • Food security is improving: undernourishment fell from 9.1 to 7.8 per cent between 2023 and 2025, and the share of people unable to afford a healthy diet fell from 37.4 to 32.7 per cent between 2021 and 2025

Remaining Gaps

  • Emissions intensity for manure management increased between 2022 and 2023 and held flat for soil fertilization
  • Food loss held at 13 per cent of production between 2021 and 2023
  • There are currently no global indicators tracking the resilience of food systems to climate shocks or the finance reaching small-scale farmers

Axis 4. Building Resilience for Cities, Infrastructure and Water

Of the 20 global indicators under this axis: 3 show progress | 1 shows no progress | 16 have insufficient data

Global Progress

  • Cities are increasingly central to national climate plans, as the number of NDCs with strong urban content doubled from 52 to 104 (2024–2026)
  • The number of cities with people-centred adaptation measures rose from 308 to 392 between 2024 and 2025
  • The number of countries with multi-hazard early warning systems rose from 108 to 119 between 2024 and 2025

Remaining Gaps

  • The number of people living in water-stressed basins is higher in 2025 than it was in 2015
  • Globally comparable evidence on the climate resilience of sanitation infrastructure and services remains limited, highlighting a critical gap in measuring adaptation implementation and outcomes
  • On zero waste, only 6.9 per cent of material inputs came from secondary materials in 2021, and only 22.3 per cent of e-waste was formally recycled in 2022

Axis 5. Fostering Human and Social Development

Of the 10 global indicators under this axis: 5 show progress | 1 shows no progress | 4 have insufficient data

Global Progress

  • Renewable energy jobs reached 16.6 million in 2024 (up from 16.2 million in 2023)
  • Extreme poverty fell from 831 million people to 811 million between 2025 and 2026, while the number of people experiencing hunger decreased from ~659 million to ~645 million between 2024 and 2025
  • The number of developing countries with updated National Adaptation Plans (NAPs) rose from 59 to 81 between 2024 and 2026

Remaining Gaps

  • Women’s share of renewable energy jobs stayed stagnant at 32 per cent between 2024 and 2025
  • Comparable global indicators remain limited for climate education, adaptation skills and workforce readiness, as well as global monitoring for the protection of cultural heritage, traditional knowledge and cultural practices against climate risks

Axis 6. Unleashing Enablers and Accelerators, including on Finance, Technology and Capacity Building

Of the 20 global indicators under this axis: 9 show progress | 4 show no progress | 7 have insufficient data

Global Progress

  • Climate finance passed USD 2 trillion in 2024, with progress both in global public and private finance
  • The number of countries with green public procurement frameworks increased from 49 in 2022 to 54 in 2025
  • Developing countries receiving NAP support rose from 89 to 92 between 2024 and 2026 and readiness funding grew from USD 642 million to USD 749 million between 2024 and 2025

Remaining Gaps

  • Adaptation finance is fluctuating, and decreased from USD 81.5 billion in 2022 to USD 64.1 billion in 2024
  • Non-CO₂ emissions are off track: energy methane stayed flat at 144 Mt between 2024 and 2025, and agricultural N₂O rose from 7.46 to 7.57 Mt between 2022 and 2023
